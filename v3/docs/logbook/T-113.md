## Surprised
**The R2 assertion took four attempts and every failure taught something.**

- *Attempt 1* — per-merchant max week-over-week change against the merchant's own
  pre-onset sigma: **8.46σ**. Not R2 ramping; arrival noise. At Fano 12.25 a single
  merchant's weekly GMV swings several sigma by itself.
- *Attempt 2* — raw cohort mean: **6.78σ**. Worse, and for an embarrassing reason. The
  cohort mean is dominated by L4 and L8 merchants whose tickets are two orders of
  magnitude above L1's, while the median baseline sigma describes a typical small
  merchant. The ratio was measuring the *persona mix*, not the ramp.
- *Attempt 3* — normalise each merchant by its own pre-onset mean, then take the cohort
  mean: **2.56σ**. Better, still wrong: dividing by a noisy small baseline produces heavy
  tails that drag a mean around on their own.
- *Attempt 4* — cohort **median** of normalised tracks, restricted to merchants observed
  across the whole 9-week window, and the **fitted slope** rather than the largest observed
  step: **0.66σ**. Passes with margin.

**The lesson is the one this project keeps relearning: at Fano 12.25 almost every naive
statistic measures the arrival process.** Three of those four attempts produced a number
that looked like a finding about R2 and was actually a finding about overdispersion. The
final test asserts the *systematic* increment and reports the observed maximum beside it,
so the gap between the two is visible rather than hidden by the choice of estimator.

The companion test is the one that actually states the claim operationally: R2's
week-over-week log-change distribution is statistically close to a legitimate merchant's.
That is why R2 is hard, and it is why per-typology recall has to be reported separately —
an aggregate that R1's easy wins carry would hide it completely.
