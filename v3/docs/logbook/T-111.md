## Surprised
**Two things, and the second one cost the most.**

1. numpy's `negative_binomial(n, p)` has Fano exactly `1/p`, independent of `n`. So the
   dispersion parameter is a pure function of the *target* and `n` carries lambda, which
   makes the calibration exact at every intensity rather than approximate. The spec's
   `r = lambda / (F - 1)` and numpy's parameterisation agree, but only once you notice
   that `p` is the knob and `n` is the payload.

2. **The per-merchant-vs-pooled choice for `fano_factor` is load-bearing and it is not
   obvious.** Pooling every merchant's days into one variance folds the *between*-merchant
   intensity spread into the statistic: a population of perfectly Poisson merchants at
   different rates reports a Fano far above 1 and the number stops measuring the arrival
   process at all. `test_fano_ignores_between_merchant_spread` exists because I got this
   wrong on paper first.

**And then the same mistake reappeared one level up, in G1.** Composing the intensity
(persona shape x typology ramp x day-of-week x confounder) makes a merchant's *own* rate
non-stationary, and by the law of total variance that adds `Var(lambda)/E[lambda]` on top
of whatever the count process supplies. Drawing at a flat `F_nb = 12.25` produced a
realised population Fano of **15.11** and G1 was correctly RED. The fix is
`nb_fano_for_target`: solve per merchant for `F_nb = target - Var(lambda)/E[lambda]`, so
the *composed* series lands on the target. That is also the right reading of the v1
measurement — 12.25 was measured on real merchant counts, which already contain real
seasonality, real growth and real platform events.

## Broke
`test_arrivals.py` passed 17/17 on the first run, which turned out to be a false comfort:
it isolates the process at a constant intensity, which is exactly the condition under
which the composed-variance bug is invisible. The gate found it. The unit test was not
wrong, it was answering a narrower question than the one that mattered, and it still is —
it is now paired with G1a, which measures what the dataset actually has.
