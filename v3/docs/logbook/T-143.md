Zero of five seeds improve PR-AUC. One of five improves savings, and it is seed 46 — the same
seed on which Rung 3 was worst, so the "win" is Rung 3 regressing rather than Rung 4
improving. **Rung 4 is dropped from the scoring path** (Prime Directive 5). The code and its
tests stay in the tree; a deleted negative result is a negative result nobody can check.

Two metrics did improve and they are worth naming rather than hiding: precision@K rises to
0.2311 (best of any rung) and recall@K to 0.1196. Weighting the positives by exposure makes
the model prefer large merchants, which raises the hit rate on a size-correlated positive
class — and then loses money anyway, because d30 halves (0.1176 → 0.0588) and the alerts land
later. **That is the case for FR-021's rule that savings and the floors travel on the same row
as the ranking metrics**, and it is the single most useful thing this P2 ticket produced.

## Built

`src/rakshak/models/rung4_cost.py` — `cost_weights` and a `train` that delegates to Rung 2's
fit with a `weight` vector. Rung 3's columns and Rung 3's hyperparameters, one variable
changed.

Two decisions, both in the module docstring:

- **Instance weighting, not a hand-written `fobj`.** Weighting each row makes the objective
  `sum_i w_i · logloss_i`, which *is* the cost inside the objective. A custom gradient and
  Hessian pair would put the output on a raw scale that is no longer a probability, and the
  decision layer consumes the score as a probability — so a literal custom objective would
  win the letter of the ticket and lose what the ticket is for.
- **The weight is an exposure estimate, never a realised loss.** `true_loss_amount_inr` is
  radioactive under Prime Directive 3 and is also unavailable in production, where nobody
  knows the loss until it lands. `w = clip(exposure / review_cost, 1, 50)` on positives, 1 on
  negatives. The cap is not cosmetic: exposure is lognormal over three orders of magnitude,
  and uncapped the top few merchants would outweigh the rest of the positive class combined.

## Surprised

**Cost weighting is the wrong medicine for a positive class of eight merchants.** The
literature's claim is that putting the cost inside the fit spends capacity where being wrong
is expensive. That argument assumes there is enough positive signal to redistribute. With 960
positive rows from 8 merchants, the exposure weights concentrate the fit onto the largest two
or three of those eight, and the effective sample size falls further. The mechanism is real;
the precondition is not met here.

Worth stating for the writeup rather than as a defence: this is a negative result **about
this dataset**, not about Bahnsen-style example-dependent cost-sensitive learning. Under a
label regime with hundreds of resolved positives the answer could easily go the other way,
and nothing measured here says otherwise.
