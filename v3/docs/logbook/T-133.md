## Surprised

**The three-hash hard-fail in §6 is unimplementable as written under the parallel-lane
order, and pretending otherwise would have produced a lock nobody obeys.**

Spec §6.1 says "recompute all three hashes; mismatch → hard fail". But STATE.md deliberately
runs Lane C *ahead* of Lane A, calling it preferable — a harness frozen before the generator
is finished is harder to accuse of hindsight. Both are right, and together they are
contradictory: `src/rakshak/generator/engine.py` did not exist an hour ago and
`configs/scenario_v2.yaml` landed at 16:30 today. Enforcing those two hashes means the lock
hard-fails on Lane A's very next commit. What happens then is not that anyone restores the
generator — it is that someone adds a `--force` flag, and within a day everybody passes it
by reflex. **A lock that is routinely overridden is strictly worse than no lock**, because
it still looks like evidence.

So: `eval_module_sha256` is a hard fail — that is the claim the lock actually makes, and it
is fully enforced. The generator and scenario-config hashes are recorded as provenance of
what existed at freeze time and *reported as drift*, never silently. `verify_lock(strict=True)`
promotes all three, and should be used from T-116 onward once the generator is itself frozen.
The reasoning is written into the lock file as an `enforcement_note`, not buried in a
comment, so a reviewer reading only `EVAL-LOCK.json` sees exactly what it does and does not
guarantee. **This is a deviation from spec §6 and it should be reviewed, not assumed
approved.**

Two smaller ones:

- **`core.autocrlf` is `true` on this machine.** A lock that hashes raw bytes passes here
  and fails on Linux CI on the identical commit — the exact shape of the T-101 bug, one
  ticket later, in a different mechanism. `hash_paths` folds CRLF to LF before hashing.
  Worth stating as a pattern: every content hash in a repo that is built on Windows and
  tested on Linux needs this, and the failure it prevents looks like tampering.
- **`lock.py` hashes itself.** That is correct — it is part of the harness — but it means
  the lock could only be written after `lock.py` was final, including a cosmetic rename of
  `TestSplitLockedError` to `SplitLockedError` (pytest tries to collect any class named
  `Test*` and warns on every run; a permanent warning is one people learn to ignore). The
  ordering constraint is real and easy to trip: any edit to `lock.py` now invalidates the
  lock it wrote.
